## Chapter 4: What Is a GPU?

Chapter four. What is a G P U? And do I need one?

Every computer has a brain chip called a C P U. A C P U is like four to twelve master chefs. Each chef is super clever. They can cook anything, follow tricky recipes, and decide what to do next. But there aren't many of them. A G P U is like thousands of kitchen helpers. Each helper is simple. They can only do one small job, like chopping one carrot. But there are thousands of them, and they all chop at the same time! G P U stands for Graphics Processing Unit. It was first made for video games. To draw a game, the computer colours millions of tiny dots on the screen, sixty times every second. Each dot is a simple job, so thousands of helpers are perfect. Remember, a model is a recipe of five hundred million numbers. To make one sound token, the computer multiplies your words by most of those numbers. To make one second of speech, it does that about twenty-five times. That's billions of multiplications, every second. But each multiplication is easy. Zero point three seven, times one point two. A simple, carrot-chopping job. On a C P U, the master chefs do them a few at a time, and a sentence could take ages. On a G P U, thousands of helpers do them all at once, and the sentence comes out faster than you can say it. And training, the guess, check, nudge loop, is even hungrier. You do those billions of multiplications forwards, then backwards to work out the nudges, millions of times. Without a G P U, training would take years. Most A I is built for one family of G P Us, made by a company called NVIDIA. NVIDIA G P Us speak a special language called CUDA, and a lot of A I code expects it. When we started, we checked our own computers. The Mac had no NVIDIA G P U. The Windows P C only had a small built-in graphics chip. So for training, we had to rent an NVIDIA G P U over the internet. But here's some exciting news. New Macs have very powerful chips, like Apple's M five family. And some have lots of memory, like forty-eight gigabytes. Apple's chips have a clever trick called unified memory. On most computers, the C P U and the G P U each have their own memory, like two kids with two lunchboxes. On a Mac, they share one big lunchbox. So a Mac with forty-eight gigabytes can fit a really big model into that shared memory. It can run lots of open models, and small experiments, like testing a voice clone from a fifteen-second clip, without renting a G P U. Apple even has a free toolbox for this, called M L X. So why would you still rent a G P U? Training is far heavier than running. If a hundred people press play at the same moment, your one Mac can't serve them all. Some tools only work on NVIDIA. And your Mac needs to be off sometimes! Here's the simple rule. Play and learn on your Mac. Train, and serve lots of people, on a rented G P U.

## Chapter 9: LoRA: Sticky Notes for Your Model

Chapter nine. LoRA. Sticky notes for your model.

Imagine Chatterbox is a giant encyclopedia about talking. It's five hundred million numbers thick. And you want it to learn one new thing. How your voice talks. You could rewrite the whole encyclopedia. That's called full fine tuning. It's slow, expensive, and you might accidentally rub out good stuff it already knew. Or, you could add a few sticky notes. That's LoRA. It stands for low rank adaptation. Big words, simple idea. Freeze the big model, so none of its numbers change. Add tiny sticky notes next to some of its pages. And only train the sticky notes. When the model runs, it reads each page, plus the sticky note, and the notes gently nudge it towards your voice. Why low rank? Inside the model, many pages are big grids of numbers. Say one thousand rows, by one thousand columns. That's a million numbers in one grid. A LoRA note doesn't add a whole new million number grid. It adds two skinny strips. One that's a thousand by thirty-two, and one that's thirty-two by a thousand. Multiply them together, and they make a full size change! But the strips only have sixty-four thousand numbers. About fifteen times smaller. The thirty-two is called the rank, or r. A small rank means small, simple notes. A big rank means big, detailed notes. In Chatterbox, we put sticky notes on the attention parts of the brain. Attention is how the model decides which earlier words and sounds to look at, while it makes the next sound. Like when you read, the cat sat on the, and your brain looks back at cat, to guess mat. We started with a free, open source training kit on GitHub, called chatterbox fine tuning. Someone else had already written the hard code! We fed it the list of clips and words, rented an R T X forty ninety, and trained the sticky notes. An epoch means reading every clip once. The model reads all the clips a few times, then saves the notes as a small file. The first two voices took about two and a half minutes of training each. Here's an honest story. Our first LoRAs were too big, and trained too long. The voices learned to sound like one flat podcast. Very samey, and they couldn't laugh or get excited. So they fixed the recipe. Smaller notes, rank thirty-two instead of a hundred and twenty-eight. Fewer reads, three epochs instead of ten. Gentler nudges. Notes only on attention. Keep the umms in the written words. And better data, thirty to sixty minutes of lively, acted speech. And a blind test. Listen to the LoRA voice and the fifteen-second voice without knowing which is which, and only keep the LoRA if it wins. The lesson? Sticky notes should nudge, not shout. Too much training makes a model forget what it knew. That's called overfitting. Like memorising one page of a test, instead of learning the subject. Because LoRA notes are small, you can keep lots of them, and swap them in and out of one big model. We had about seventeen voice LoRAs, sharing one Chatterbox brain. Is a LoRA code? Not quite. A LoRA is a little file of numbers, a mini set of weights. A small piece of code loads it, and adds it to the big model. And there are ways to tweak a voice without any training at all. The feelings knob, exaggeration. C F G weight, how closely it follows the recording. Tags like chuckle and sigh. And stage directions, like whispering, or excited.

## Chapter 12: How Computers Hear Music

Chapter twelve. How computers hear music. Sound waves, and Spotify's Basic Pitch.

Chatterbox isn't the only treasure. There are thousands of open models, and some of the coolest ones are about music. To understand them, we first need to understand sound itself. So let's zoom in. Way in. When you press a piano key, a tiny hammer hits a string, and the string wiggles. It pushes the air: squish, stretch, squish, stretch. That's a sound wave! How fast it wiggles is called the frequency. Slow wiggles make low notes. Fast wiggles make high notes. And middle C? It wiggles about two hundred and sixty times, every second! The name hertz comes from a scientist called Heinrich Hertz, who, in the eighteen eighties, was the first person to make and detect invisible radio waves. The lowest piano key wiggles about twenty-seven times a second. The highest, over four thousand! Go up one octave, from one C to the next C, and the wiggles double. The note A that orchestras tune to wiggles exactly four hundred and forty times a second. And how big the wiggles are is how loud the sound is. Here's a secret. A piano note isn't one clean wiggle. The string wiggles as a whole, which gives the main note we hear, called the fundamental. But it also wiggles in halves, thirds and quarters, at the same time, making quieter, higher wiggles called overtones. So when you play middle C, there are also quiet wiggles at about two, three, and four times the main note, all mixed in. The mix of overtones is a big part of why a piano and a guitar sound different, even on exactly the same note. Now press three keys at once. A chord. You don't get three separate waves flying through the air. You get one single, tangled up wave, with all three notes and all their overtones mixed together. So here's the puzzle. Given one tangled wave, which notes are hiding inside it? It's like hearing a whole choir, and trying to write down every singer's note. That's really hard, and it's exactly what Basic Pitch does. Basic Pitch is an open model made by Spotify, the music app. You give it a recording, like you singing, a guitar or a piano, and it writes down every note. Which note, when it started, how long it lasted, and how loud it was. It saves the answer as MIDI. MIDI is like sheet music for computers. It doesn't store the sound. It stores instructions, like, play middle C now, for half a second, medium loud. Hum a tune, and turn it into a trumpet! How does Basic Pitch listen? Step one. Make a picture of the sound, called a spectrogram. Time goes across, low and high notes go up and down, and loud parts glow brighter. It uses a special kind called a C Q T, where the rows are spaced like piano keys. Three rows for every key, so it can see notes bending. Step two. Stack the harmonics, like pancakes. It makes copies of the picture, shifted up by the overtone amounts, and stacks them. Now a real note and all its overtones line up in the same spot, and real notes glow extra bright. Step three. A small brain looks at the stack. It's a convolutional neural network, a model that slides little magnifying glasses across a picture, looking for patterns. The same kind of model that spots cats in photos. About eighty-six times a second, it makes three guesses. Onset: is a note starting right now? Note: which notes are sounding? And pitch: exactly how high is it, including slides and bends? Then some normal code joins the guesses into notes, and saves a MIDI file. How did Spotify build it? With the same guess, check, nudge loop! The trick is finding music where you already know every right note. Recordings from self playing pianos that write down every key press. Guitar recordings with the notes marked. Singing with the notes written out. Because it learned from many instruments, it works on almost any instrument. And the amazing part: Basic Pitch is tiny. Chatterbox has about five hundred million numbers. Basic Pitch has fewer than twenty thousand. So it runs fast on any laptop or phone, even inside a web browser, and it doesn't need a G P U at all. Spotify shared it with the Apache licence, on GitHub. So who used it? In the next chapter, we'll meet a real app that did.

## Chapter 16: How Models Are Really Made

Chapter sixteen. How models are really made.

Before you go, let's peek behind the curtain. Imagine you're the founder of a team building an open source text to video model. You type a sentence, like a cat surfing a giant wave, and it makes a video. How would you build that? First, you need to know how a computer sees. A picture is a grid of tiny dots, called pixels. And each pixel is just three numbers: how much red, how much green, and how much blue, from zero to two hundred and fifty-five. A full HD picture has about two million pixels. That's six million numbers. A video is twenty-four pictures every second. So a five second video is a hundred and twenty pictures, about seven hundred and fifty million numbers! To a computer, a video is just a giant block of numbers. Words become numbers too. A helper model, called a text encoder, reads your sentence, a cat surfing a giant wave, and turns its meaning into a long list of numbers. So now the whole problem is numbers in, numbers out. Here's the big trick most video models use, called diffusion. Take a real video, and add a little fuzzy static. Then a bit more. And more. Until it's nothing but static, like an old TV. Then you teach the model to do the opposite. Look at a fuzzy picture, guess the static that was added, and take a little bit away. Guess, check, nudge, millions of times, until it gets really good at cleaning up fuzz. Now the magic. To make a brand new video, you start with pure static, plus your sentence's numbers. The model cleans a little fuzz away. Then a little more. After about thirty to fifty steps, a cat on a surfboard appears out of the fog! Like a sculptor finding a statue inside a block of marble. There's one more shortcut. Videos are too big to work on directly. So another helper model squishes each video into a tiny zipped up version, about fifty times smaller. The model does all its cleaning in that tiny version, and then the helper un-squishes it back into pixels at the end. And to keep the cat looking like the same cat in every frame, the video gets chopped into tiny cubes, across space and time. A transformer, the same attention brain we met before, looks at how all the cubes relate. This cube is the cat's ear, and so is that one, one frame later. Now, where do the training videos come from? A model like this learns from millions of short clips, each with a description. Teams use licensed stock footage, videos with open licences, public research collections, and videos they film themselves. Where the data comes from is a really important question. ## Chapter 17: The Giant Machine Behind AI

Chapter seventeen. The giant machine behind A I.

In the last chapter, the model guessed, checked and nudged, millions of times. So here's a great question. Does a person sit there and correct it, millions of times? No way! That would take forever. Here's the clever bit. Remember, we added the static ourselves. So we already know the right answer! It's like a worksheet with the answer key printed at the back. The computer compares the model's guess to the answer key, and gives it a score, called the loss. Big loss, very wrong. Small loss, nearly right. No person needed. Then comes the nudge. A bit of maths called backpropagation works backwards through the model, and asks every single number, did you help cause this mistake? Then it turns each one a tiny bit, the way that makes the loss smaller. Billions of numbers, all nudged at once. One round of guess, check, nudge takes about a second. Then it happens again. And again. A big training run can do it hundreds of thousands of times, on millions and millions of videos. That's where the millions come from. So where do people come in? At the end, people make thousands of choices, not millions. Then the team trains a small helper model, a judge, to learn what people like. The judge can then score millions of videos automatically, because it learned people's taste. So what code did the humans actually write? Here's the surprise. They don't write the backwards maths by hand. PyTorch works that out automatically. The humans write all the instructions around it. There's the data code, that downloads, cuts, cleans and captions the clips. The model code, that snaps the blocks together. The training loop. The teamwork code, that splits the job across thousands of G P Us. And the testing code, that makes sample videos and draws the loss line, so people can watch. That teamwork code is really clever. Each G P U gets different videos, and works out its own nudges. Then they all share their nudges over super fast cables, and agree on one average nudge. Then everyone takes the same step together. Like a big class marking different pages of homework, and then comparing notes. Now, where do all those videos live? Not on one laptop! They're uploaded to giant online storage, called buckets, like Amazon S three, or Google Cloud Storage. A bucket can hold almost any number of files, and you pay a little for every gigabyte you keep there. Video collections are enormous. They're measured in petabytes. One petabyte is a million gigabytes. That's about fifteen thousand phones, completely full! And big video models can learn from many petabytes. That's so much data, that sending it over the internet can take weeks. So sometimes, companies actually pack hard drives into boxes, and ship them in a truck!
