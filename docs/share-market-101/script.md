## Introduction

[STORY: Alice walks through her day as a stick figure. Every object she touches lights up with a little price tag and a company logo bubble: her phone, her school bag, the bus, the café, the supermarket.]

When people think about investing in the share market, they think of gambling, or a guy staring at four screens full of scary charts. It's none of that. Investing is simply buying a small piece of a business. If the business makes more money over time, the share price tends to follow, and you can make money too. But here's the catch: you need to buy at the right price!

Businesses are growing and shrinking around us all the time. All we need to do is look. Most things we use every day have a business behind them. Your furniture at home. Your clothes, your phone, your laptop, your internet provider. Your house, your school, your car. The shopping mall, the roads, the office buildings, the hospitals, the bridges and tunnels you drive through. Social media, streaming, food delivery, ride hailing. The grocery store, and the food inside the grocery store. Restaurants. Almost everything is a business, and there's fierce competition everywhere.

Some businesses have just a few competitors, and those don't change often. Think of the telco that gives you home wifi, your hospital, or your choice of car. Other businesses have lots of substitutes, like cafes, restaurants and clothing brands. Some companies have been around for decades, like Visa, Mastercard and the big banks. Others are less than twenty years old, like Uber and Airbnb. Our goal is to find big sectors that are growing, ideally a sector where there are two or three winners, and buy them at the right price. We'll talk about how.

A lot of the companies around us are private, which makes them hard for us to buy into. Ikea, for example, is owned by foundations set up by its founder's family, so we can't just buy a piece of it. Our focus is publicly listed companies. With a trading platform, we can buy a piece of those. In this course we'll walk step by step through how a parent can set up an account for you, and we'll build a portfolio of stocks together.

You don't need much to start. Ask your parents, or a family friend, if they'd be okay putting in a small amount, say a hundred dollars, that everyone agrees you might lose while you learn. Because if you don't invest, you'll never really learn.

In Google's case, twenty-five times seemed reasonable then, and still does for a business that has long dominated search. Although that dominance is now being challenged, by US courts that ruled it acted as a monopoly, and by new AI chatbots.

## Chapter 1 – Learn How to Learn

[SCREEN: Big letters drop in one at a time: M… E… F… E.]

Before we get into markets, stocks or money, we need to talk about how to learn. We use what we call the MEFE method.

**M is for Micro learning, every day.** The best way to learn is a little bit every day. Fifteen minutes a day beats three hours once a week. On day one, fifteen minutes won't feel like much. But two weeks in, you'll think, "Hey, I've learned quite a bit." A month in, you'll know more than your friends. Three months in, you'll know as much as most adults, maybe more. Like investing, learning compounds over time. Take your time and watch at your own pace, but don't skip ahead until you understand what you're watching.

## Chapter 2 – Why Invest?

[STORY: Alice finds a crisp stack of $1,000 and stuffs it under her mattress. A clock spins forward ten years. She pulls it out, still $1,000, but the shopping trolley it fills is noticeably emptier.]

Why not just keep your money in the bank? Why bother investing? Let's explore.

Say you find a thousand dollars and hide it under your mattress for ten years. Ten years later, you still have your thousand dollars. But it buys less than it would today. It's the same amount of money, but it's WORTH less, because you can buy fewer things with it. Twenty years ago, a hundred dollars bought a lot more at the grocery store than it does today. That's because prices go up, almost every year. It's called inflation.

### Concept #1 — Inflation

Inflation is the rate at which the prices of goods and services rise over time. The US Federal Reserve targets about two percent a year, and Australia's Reserve Bank targets two to three percent. At two percent a year, prices double roughly every thirty-six years. Put the other way round, the purchasing power of cash halves about every thirty-six years. At three percent, it's every twenty-four years! And right now inflation is running above target. The IMF expects about three and a half percent in the US this year, and nearly four in Australia.

> ✏️ Fact-check: was "Central banks like the US Federal Reserve and Australia's Reserve Bank target roughly 2% per year"; now the Fed targets 2% and the RBA targets 2–3% (RBA inflation explainer, rba.gov.au). Added current IMF 2026 inflation forecasts: US 3.6%, Australia 3.9% (IMF WEO, Oct 2026).

Are prices really going up? Yes, they are. You don't notice it day to day, but look back ten years and you'll see how much cheaper things were.

The official measure of inflation is the Consumer Price Index, or CPI. Officials needed a single measure to track inflation, so they bundled together a basket of goods and services, and that gives us one number: CPI. That way they can say "inflation is four percent" or "three percent", instead of reporting price changes across hundreds of different things. This simplification has flaws, but it's what's accepted for now, so we'll go with it. For example, in the US, "core inflation" leaves out food and energy because their prices bounce around too much. Those count as "non-core". Honestly, I can't think of anything more core than food and energy! In Australia, the Reserve Bank watches something called the "trimmed mean", which cuts out the biggest price jumps and drops each quarter.

> ➕ Added: Australia's trimmed-mean measure, since Australian kids will hear it in the news (RBA).

CPI is a complex formula. You can ask Claude to explain how CPI is calculated in the US versus Australia and other markets. The truth is, we don't need those details. What we need to know is that things around us keep getting more expensive. The cash sitting in our bank accounts is losing purchasing power. We'll be able to buy fewer things with the same money in the future. Going to a job and earning a salary isn't enough. We need our money to work for us and make more money.

What we need to understand is that inflation means the price of food goes up. And housing. And labour. And energy bills. Things get more expensive. ## Chapter 3 – The Magic of Compounding

[STORY: Alice rolls a tiny snowball down a hill. It picks up snow, gets bigger, picks up even more snow, and ends up the size of a house.]

We know prices are rising. Cash is slowly losing value. We need to make our money work for us.

### Concept #2 — Compounding

So we know prices around us rise almost every year. Our money is worth less and less. We need to put it to work, not just leave it sitting in the bank, slowly losing its worth. So what can we do?

Here comes Concept number two: the magic of compounding.

If you made twenty-five percent a year, which is very hard to do, you'd turn every dollar into about nine dollars in ten years, and about eighty-seven dollars in twenty years. Let's open a spreadsheet and test it out.

Compounding means earning returns not just on the money you put in, but on your returns too. A thousand dollars invested at ten percent a year becomes eleven hundred after year one. In year two, you earn ten percent on eleven hundred, not a thousand, which gives you twelve hundred and ten. After thirty years: about seventeen thousand four hundred and forty-nine dollars, from a one-thousand-dollar start. You didn't save seventeen thousand dollars. Time did it for you.

But it's hard to make twenty-five percent consistently. So if we make ten percent, let's see when we ten-x our money. The answer is about twenty-four years. Slower, but still magic.

> ➕ Added: the missing answer to "If we make 10%, let's see when we 10x" (≈24.2 years, since 1.1^24 ≈ 9.85 and 1.1^25 ≈ 10.8).

So with compounding, time is your friend. The longer you invest, the more you can compound. And it isn't just finance. Learning compounds too. The more time you put into art, music, sport or anything you love, the better you get.

## Chapter 4 – Where Should I Invest, and What Can I Expect?

[STORY: Alice stands at a giant "Investing Food Court" with a stall for each asset class: Shares, Private Equity, Bonds, Property, Gold, and a flashy neon "Crypto & Other" stall.]

There are lots of places to put money. These are called asset classes.

**Listed, or public, shares (also called equities).** A small ownership stake in a company. If the company grows its profits, the share price tends to follow. Over the long run, shares have had the highest returns of any major asset class. They're also the most up-and-down.

**Private equity and venture capital.** Funds that invest in private companies. Venture capital, or VC, backs early-stage companies: higher risk, higher potential reward. Private equity, or PE, buys later-stage companies, with more modest but more predictable returns. Everyday investors generally can't access these directly.

**Bonds.** You lend money to a government or a company, and they pay you interest. Lower risk, lower return. Right now, a thirty-year US Treasury bond pays about five point three percent a year, the highest it's been in about twenty years.

> ✏️ Fact-check: was "The 30-year US Treasury bond currently yields around 4–5%"; now 5.32% (US Treasury, 29 Oct 2026).

**Real estate.** Strong long-run returns, especially when bought with a loan. But it's hard to sell quickly, costs a lot to get into, needs ongoing maintenance, and is very sensitive to interest rates.

**Gold.** A store of value. It doesn't pay you any income, but over very long periods it has held on to its purchasing power. You can see gold's last hundred years on Macrotrends.

## Chapter 5 – Risk versus return

[STORY: Alice on a playground. A short, safe slide has a sign reading "Small thrill, safe". A giant twisty slide has a sign reading "Big thrill, could bump your head".]

One of the most important ideas in finance is risk and reward. If you take on higher risk, you should expect a higher return. Otherwise, why not just buy the risk-free asset?

Let's say you stumble on a million dollars. What would you do with it? Put it all on one stock? Buy property? This is where we meet risk and reward.

If you're twenty, you might have a bigger appetite for risk, and you have time on your side for your wealth to compound. You might put a big chunk into a low-cost index fund, like one that tracks the S&P 500 in the US. Over long periods, it has historically grown about ten percent a year on average, though some years it falls a lot, like thirty-seven percent in 2008. Then you let time work the magic of compounding. If you can handle more risk, you might buy some individual stocks, hoping to ten-x your money or find the next Tesla or Nvidia, even if you lose some along the way. Most stock picks won't do that. If you want a higher return, you have to be able to handle a higher risk of losing money.

> ✏️ Fact-check: was "watch your money grow 10-11% a year"; now "historically about ten percent a year on average… some years it falls a lot" (S&P 500 long-run ~10% nominal with dividends; 2008 −37%, S&P Dow Jones Indices). Not a guarantee.

But if you're eighty, you might not have the same appetite for risk. You'd likely accept a lower return in exchange for lower risk. At eighty, you might want mature, steady companies that pay a dividend. You won't one-hundred-x your money, but you could get cash flow and live off the dividends. That, in a nutshell, is risk and reward.

Stocks are risky by nature. They go up and down. If you want zero risk, the closest thing is lending to the US government. A thirty-year US Treasury bond is paying about five point three percent right now.

Most governments around the world borrow money by issuing bonds. You and I can buy them too. It's called "risk-free" because we're counting on the US government not going bankrupt, and paying us back. We get rewarded for lending them money: they pay us interest. One small catch: if interest rates rise, a long bond's price can fall before it matures. That's why experts often treat short-term Treasury bills as the "purest" risk-free rate.

> ✏️ Fact-check: was "a 30 year treasury bond that's currently giving around 4%… We get 4% without taking any risk"; now 5.32% (US Treasury, 29 Oct 2026). Added the interest-rate-risk nuance, since a 30-year bond is default-free but not price-risk-free.

BUT, let's say you need to make more money. A million dollars isn't enough to buy a house in many cities. You're young and willing to take a risk, and you want a higher return. Let's look at an example.

[STORY: Alice's friend Leo bursts in with a sketch of a bubble tea shop. "Give me $1 million for half the shop!" A money bag hovers between two thought bubbles: one shows a busy store with a "SOLD to big chain!" banner, the other an empty shop with a "Closed" sign.]

Say a friend comes to you and says, "I want to start a bubble tea shop." They want a million dollars for fifty percent of the business. They'll use the money to rent a store, buy equipment and ingredients, and spend a little on hiring and marketing. The risk: your million dollars could go to zero. The upside: the business takes off, becomes profitable, and there's a chance it gets bought out or goes public.

The question to ask yourself is how much return you need to take on that risk, knowing you could lose all your money. Is it doubling your money in five years, which is about fifteen percent a year? Is it thirty percent a year? That extra return is called your risk premium: how much more you expect than the risk-free rate, which is guaranteed, to put your money at risk.

That is the foundation of all capital allocation.

### What is the share market?

The share market is a marketplace that lets you buy a small ownership stake in a publicly listed company. If you own a hundred shares of Google, you're legally a part-owner of Alphabet Inc.

Most companies are private. When a company reaches a certain size, it can list on a stock exchange through an Initial Public Offering, or IPO. After that, anyone can buy or sell its shares. In the US, the two main exchanges are the New York Stock Exchange and the Nasdaq. Tech companies tend to list on the Nasdaq. The regulator is the SEC. In Australia, it's the ASX, regulated by ASIC.

### Stocks vs ETFs

A stock is a share in one company. An ETF, or Exchange-Traded Fund, is a basket of stocks. Buy one, and you own a little bit of many companies at once.

ETF fees matter enormously. Some funds that give you US shares charge half a percent a year or more, while the cheapest S&P 500 ETFs charge only about three or four hundredths of a percent, whether they're listed in Australia or the US. Always check the management expense ratio, or MER. Choose the lowest-cost ETF and the lowest-cost broker, and watch out for currency conversion fees.

### Why do companies go public?

Going public lets early shareholders turn paper wealth into real money. It also gives companies access to large, cheaper pools of money, a "stock currency" they can use to buy other companies, and shares they can offer to attract great staff. The trade-off: reporting every quarter, public scrutiny, and pressure to deliver short-term earnings. Interestingly, the number of listed companies in the US has fallen from about eight thousand in the late 1990s to under four thousand today. Companies are staying private longer.

### Company filings: the source of truth

In the US, company filings live on the SEC's EDGAR website. A 10-K is the annual report. A 10-Q is the quarterly report. An 8-K covers big events, like acquisitions or a new CEO. And a Form 4 shows insiders buying and selling shares. In Australia, look at announcements on the ASX website.

### Why should we buy a stock?

Because we want a return for investing. Here's the simple formula: share price equals earnings times the multiple. If earnings go up, over time the share price tends to follow.

That's why, when a company decides to go public, its job is to deliver a good return for its investors. It's not a written rule, but that's the point of the markets: create wealth for your investors. A listed company has to report earnings every quarter, and its job is to grow revenue and profit to build a bigger business.

Most listed companies won't grow their earnings by more than fifteen percent a year for the next decade. We need to try to find the ones that will. They might launch new products, open more stores, expand into new countries, cut costs, refinance their debt. There are many things companies can do, and we'll talk about them.

## Chapter 14 – The Three Financial Statements

[STORY: Hook. Alice opens a lemonade stand. At the end of the day she has three questions: "Did I make money today?" (Profit and Loss). "What do I own and what do I owe?" (Balance sheet). "Where did my actual cash go?" (Cash flow). Three little notebooks appear in her hands.]

Every public company has to publish three financial statements: the profit and loss statement, the balance sheet, and the cash flow statement.

The good thing about investing is that you don't need any advanced maths. All we need to do is find the right numbers and not get misled. Some companies use funny accounting to try to hide costs. We just want to look at the financial statements to get a sense of how healthy a business is. There are lots of videos online if you want to get into the details, but we'll keep it light and easy.

**1. The Profit and Loss Statement (P&L, or Income Statement)**

Revenue minus costs equals profit. The great thing about the P&L is that it really tells you about a business.

And it's still growing fast: in its latest quarter, AWS grew about thirty-nine per cent. Big market, still growing. Okay! And Netflix, have they raised prices? Yes, again in 2026.

Here's the P&L in a nutshell:

- **Revenue** equals units sold times price. The top line.
- **Cost of goods sold** is the direct cost of making the product.
- **Gross profit** equals revenue minus cost of goods sold. Gross margin is gross profit divided by revenue.
- **Operating expenses** are things like salaries, marketing, research and development, and rent.
- **EBITDA** means Earnings Before Interest, Tax, Depreciation and Amortisation. It's often used to compare businesses, but it can be dressed up.
- **Net income**, or net profit, is what's left after everything, including interest and tax. It's the "E" in the PE ratio. The most important number.

**2. The Balance Sheet**

Uber's balance sheet will show you it doesn't own factories and machinery like BMW or any car maker. It owns almost no cars at all, because it's "asset light". > ✏️ Fact-check: was "Uber owns no cars"; now "almost no cars". Since 2025 Uber has been investing in robotaxi fleets, e.g. its Lucid/Nuro deal (Uber press release, Jul 2025)

A balance sheet is a snapshot of what the company owns (assets), minus what it owes (liabilities). What's left equals shareholders' equity, also called book value.

Key things to look at:

- **Cash:** how much firepower does it have?
- **Net debt:** total debt minus cash. More meaningful than total debt on its own.
- **Goodwill:** the extra a company paid, above the value of the other company's assets, when it bought it. Very high goodwill, or big write-downs later, can be a sign of a history of overpaying for deals.
- **Capital expenditure (capex):** money spent on physical things like data centres, factories and equipment.

**Asset-light vs asset-heavy:** Uber owns almost no cars. Salesforce has no factories. Amazon has billions of dollars in fulfilment centres and data centres. BMW has enormous manufacturing plants. Asset-light businesses can grow more easily and generate more free cash flow for every dollar of sales.

**3. The Cash Flow Statement**

Cash flow is the cash that comes in (from profits, or from borrowing or selling shares), minus the cash that goes out (on factories, machinery, or buying other companies).

- **Operating cash flow:** cash from the core business.
- **Investing cash flow:** capex and buying other companies.
- **Financing cash flow:** borrowing or paying back debt, buying back shares, and paying dividends.

The most important number you can work out from it is free cash flow.

Free cash flow equals operating cash flow minus capital expenditure.

Free cash flow is harder to fiddle than net profit, and it's often a better measure of how good a business really is. A business can show an accounting profit while actually burning cash. Always look at both.

**Porter's 5 forces**

A famous Harvard professor, Michael Porter, came up with five questions that tell you how tough an industry is:
1. How easily can new competitors get in?
2. How much power do suppliers have to raise their prices?
3. How much power do customers have to push prices down?
4. Can people easily switch to a substitute, a different product that does the same job?
5. How fierce is the fight between the companies already there?

If all five are tough, it's hard to make money. Visa and Mastercard score well on almost all five. Airlines score badly on almost all five.

**Commoditised**

Is the product a commodity, where everyone's version is basically the same? Or does the company have something special that lets it charge more? Hold that thought. Next, we'll look at the special things, called moats, and then at the flip side, commoditisation.

Imagine a castle with a big moat of water around it. Attackers can't just stroll in. Great businesses have moats too. Not water — something that makes it really hard for anyone to take their customers. Let's look at six kinds.

**1. Network effects**

The product gets more valuable as more people join. Why are you on Facebook? Because your friends are on Facebook. Visa works the same way. Shops take Visa because shoppers carry it, and shoppers carry it because shops take it. Uber and Grab too: more drivers means faster pickups, faster pickups bring more riders, and more riders bring even more drivers. Round and round it goes. Once a network hits critical mass, it's almost impossible to knock off.

**2. Switching costs**

Think of big business software like Salesforce or SAP. A company might have five years of data, workflows and connections built into it. Moving away is truly painful and expensive, kind of like moving house with every cupboard glued shut. So these companies get huge pricing power, and customers keep renewing year after year.

**3. Scale and cost advantages**

Walmart buys so much stuff that it gets better prices from suppliers than any small shop ever could. Booking.com is so big it can outspend any competitor on Google ads. The bigger you are, the cheaper your inputs. And cheaper inputs help you get even bigger. It feeds itself.

**4. Brand**

Nike sneakers and no-name sneakers both protect your feet. But Nike can charge a hundred and fifty to two hundred dollars for its top shoes because of the story, the design and the dream. LVMH, the company behind Louis Vuitton, built a whole empire on this. The risk? Brands can fade. Between 2023 and 2025 Nike lost shelf space to On Running and Hoka, partly because it got complacent.

Banking licences are very hard to get. A new medicine takes ten to fifteen years and somewhere between one and a few billion dollars of clinical trials before it's approved. Walls like these keep new competitors out and protect the companies already inside. But they can also slow growth down.

## Chapter 17 – Interest Rates and Why They Affect Everything

[STORY: Alice holds a $1 coin. A time-machine dial spins to "10 years later". Her coin shrinks. Caption: "Why does money shrink when you wait?"]

Here's a secret most beginner investing courses leave out: interest rates. Understand them and a lot of things suddenly make sense. Why growth stocks fell so hard in 2022. Why house prices shot up after 2020. Why the market is willing to pay more for each dollar of earnings at some times than at others.

**The time value of money**

A dollar today is worth more than a dollar in ten years, because you can invest today's dollar and let it grow. Say the safe interest rate is five percent a year. One dollar today grows to about a dollar sixty-three in ten years. Flip that around: a dollar you'll get in ten years is only worth about sixty-one cents today.

[CHART: The shrinking future dollar — as at October 2026 | a coin shrinks as the rate slider moves from 0.5% to 5% | data: $1 in 10 yrs today = $0.95 at 0.5%; $0.82 at 2%; $0.61 at 5% (formula 1/(1+r)^10)]

That's called present value, and it's the foundation of every valuation in finance. Any asset is worth all the cash it will ever make, discounted back to today at the right rate.

[CLIP: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ks33lMoxst0 — Khan Academy, "Introduction to present value" (10:19), the bit where Sal compares $100 today with $110 next year | the cleanest explanation of present value]
[CLIP: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=733mgqrzNKs — Khan Academy, "Time value of money" (8:16), the easy walk-through | simple version for younger viewers]

**How this connects to stock prices**

Picture a fast-growing tech company. Today it earns almost nothing, but it's expected to make big profits in years five to fifteen. When interest rates are very low, say around half a percent, discounting those future profits barely shrinks them. A dollar earned in year ten is worth about ninety-five cents today. So investors happily pay a very high PE for that future.

Now raise rates to five percent. That same year-ten dollar is now worth only about sixty-one cents today. The future earnings haven't changed one bit, but they're now worth a lot less. That's why growth stocks, whose value depends on profits far in the future, fell hardest when the US Federal Reserve raised rates so quickly in 2022. The earnings didn't change. The discount rate did.

> ✏️ Fact-check: "~$0.95 when rates are near zero" made precise: that's at about 0.5% (1/1.005^10 = 0.951).

**Zero Interest Rate Policy, or ZIRP: 2009 to 2022**

After the 2008 Global Financial Crisis, the Fed cut rates to almost zero and kept them there for seven years, from late 2008 to the end of 2015. It nudged them up a little until 2018, then cut back to zero in March 2020 when COVID hit, and stayed there until March 2022. So for most of 2009 to 2022, money was super cheap. What happened? Borrowing was cheap. Prices of shares, property and crypto all rose. And growth companies whose profits were far away got very high PEs.

**The 2022 correction**

By June 2022, US inflation hit about nine percent, its highest in over forty years. The Fed raised rates from near zero to over five percent in about sixteen months. Many high-growth tech stocks fell fifty to eighty percent. The S&P 500 fell about twenty-five percent from top to bottom and finished the year down about nineteen percent. Property prices in many places softened as US mortgage rates jumped from under three percent to over seven.

In the US, the Federal Reserve sets interest rates. High rates put the brakes on the economy, because borrowing costs more. Low rates press the accelerator, because borrowing is cheap. The Fed has two official jobs: get as many people working as possible, and keep prices stable, which it defines as two percent inflation. Australia's version is the Reserve Bank of Australia, the RBA.

## Chapter 18 – How to Value a Business

[STORY: Alice at a garage sale. Two lemonade stands are for sale. One costs $10 and makes $1 a week. The other costs $1,000 and makes $2 a week. Alice raises an eyebrow.]

How do you value any business? It's the sum of all the cash it's expected to make in the future, brought back to what that's worth today.

We need a view on who's going to earn more money in the future. But we also need to make sure we're buying at a reasonable price today.

Imagine buying SpaceX at a PE of a thousand on today's earnings. We'd need to look at what it earns today, what we think it'll earn five years from now, and ten years from now. What PE would it have grown into by then? If after ten years the PE has only come down to five hundred, it's still too expensive! Starting valuation matters. Earnings growth matters.

> ✏️ Fact-check: SpaceX is private, so the PE of 1,000 is illustrative. Added "Imagine".

Our job is to find the gap between price, which is what the market charges, and value, which is what the business is really worth. When price is lower than value, that's when investors get interested.

**Method 1: The Price-to-Earnings ratio, or PE**

PE equals the company's market value divided by its yearly profit. Or, the same thing: the share price divided by earnings per share.

You can look at:

- Trailing PE: the last twelve months of real, audited earnings. This is the number I trust most, because it actually happened.
- Forward PE: what analysts expect for the next twelve months. Forecasts will be wrong, but they show what people expect right now.
- Five-year forward PE: what earnings might look like in five years. This is where most of the real work happens.

What's a fair PE? It depends on how fast the business grows and how good it is. Here are some rules of thumb:

- A slow-growing, mature business like a utility, bank or supermarket: ten to fifteen times.
- A steady compounder growing eight to twelve percent a year: twenty to twenty-five times.
- High-growth tech growing twenty-five to thirty percent a year: thirty-five to fifty times.
- An exceptional growth story: maybe fifty to a hundred times, if the market believes the growth will last.

Tesla has traded above a hundred times earnings at various points, including again in 2025 and 2026. BMW trades at around six times and Mercedes at around eight. Neither is right or wrong. They show what the market believes about the future.

> ✏️ Fact-check: "BMW and Mercedes at 5–8x" confirmed as at Oct 2026: BMW ~5.8x trailing (21 Oct 2026), Mercedes ~8x (Aug 2026 TTM) (companiesmarketcap.com).

[SCREEN: A seesaw with Tesla high in the air at "100x+" and BMW and Mercedes low at "6–8x". Alice tilts her head.]

[CLIP: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=cppxO67e6eo — Khan Academy, "Introduction to the price-to-earnings ratio" (14:46), the part where PE is built from price and EPS | PE explained]

Ben Graham said: "In the short run, the market is a voting machine; in the long run, it is a weighing machine." In the short term, emotions can push prices around. In the long term, prices drift towards what earnings can actually support.

**Method 2: Discounted Cash Flow, or DCF**

In theory, the correct way to value anything is to estimate all the cash it will ever make and bring it back to today's value.

[SCREEN: The formula types itself out: Value = CF1/(1+r)¹ + CF2/(1+r)² + … + Terminal Value. Each cash-flow coin shrinks as it travels back to "today".]

In practice:

1. Estimate revenue and free cash flow for the next five to ten years.
2. Pick a growth rate for after that, the terminal growth rate. It's usually two to four percent.
3. Discount everything at eight to twelve percent, depending on how risky the business is.
4. Add it all up. That's the intrinsic value.

DCF is powerful, but it's very sensitive to your guesses. Change the growth rate a tiny bit and the answer jumps a lot. Buffett's approach: find businesses you understand well enough that your estimate of value is roughly right. Not precisely right, roughly right. Then only buy when the price is well below that estimate.

So how do you value a house? Same idea. A house is worth what buyers can afford to pay. That depends on how many people want to live there, which is demand, how many homes are available, which is supply, and how much it costs to borrow, which is the cost of capital. When interest rates go up, buyers can borrow less, so prices usually cool.

**Method 3: Price-to-Sales and EV/EBITDA**

For companies with no profits yet, PE doesn't work. Use price-to-sales, or EV to EBITDA, instead.

EV, the enterprise value, is the market value plus net debt. It's roughly what it would cost to buy the whole company, debts and all.

Price-to-book compares the share price with the book value, which is what the company owns minus what it owes. Book value doesn't change much from year to year, so it's handy for banks and insurers, whose assets are mostly money.

**Sum-of-the-parts valuation**

For companies that do lots of different things, value each part separately. Alphabet has Google Search, which is extremely profitable, plus YouTube, Google Cloud, and early-stage "Other Bets" like Waymo. Each one deserves a different multiple.

[SCREEN: Alphabet as a pizza; each slice labelled Search, YouTube, Cloud, Other Bets gets its own price tag.]

- DuPont analysis: return on equity equals profit margin, times asset turnover, times leverage. It shows whether a company earns its returns by being efficient or by borrowing a lot.
- PEG ratio: the PE divided by the growth rate. A PE of thirty with thirty percent growth gives a PEG of one, which is often seen as fair.

## Chapter 19 – What makes a stock price go up

[STORY: Alice watches a hot-air balloon labelled "Share price". Two burners heat it: "Earnings" and "Multiple". Sandbags labelled "Fewer shares" drop off and it rises higher.]

1. **Earnings.** A share price is really just earnings multiplied by the multiple, the PE. The price by itself tells you nothing about whether a stock is cheap or expensive. A $5 stock can be expensive and a $500 stock can be cheap. What matters is how much the earnings can grow and what multiple you're paying.

2. **Buybacks.** A buyback reduces the number of shares, so each remaining share owns a bigger slice of the pie.

A buyback also raises earnings per share even when total profit stays flat. If a company earns a hundred dollars and has a hundred shares, that's a dollar a share. Buy back twenty shares and it's a dollar twenty-five a share.

3. **Returns.** Your total return is share price growth plus dividends. For example, if a share goes up six percent in a year and pays a three percent dividend, your total return is about nine percent.

4. **Other jargon: stock split.** A stock split is like cutting a pizza into more slices. There are more pieces, but it's the same pizza. Apple, Nvidia and Tesla have all split their shares. A split doesn't change what the company is worth, though it can make one share cheaper to buy.

5. **Management and capital allocation.**

When a company makes free cash flow, it has four choices.

**Choice 1: Reinvest in the business.**
If the company can earn high returns by reinvesting, through opening stores, building products, entering new markets or funding research, reinvesting is almost always best. The key question is: what's the return on invested capital, or ROIC? If a company can reinvest at twenty-five to thirty percent returns, it should reinvest nearly everything.

Amazon reinvested aggressively for decades: in warehouses and delivery, in AWS, and in Prime Video. Those investments compounded into an extraordinary business that few people could have fully predicted.

**Choice 2: Acquisitions.**
They can be life-changing. Google bought YouTube in 2006 for one point six five billion dollars, and today analysts think YouTube alone could be worth around half a trillion dollars. Or they can destroy value. Many big acquisitions never deliver the promised savings, and the buyer overpays at the top of the cycle.

**Choice 3: Dividends.**
A dividend is cash handed back to shareholders. It's like the company saying, "We have more cash than we can usefully reinvest." Mature businesses with steady cash flows, like banks, utilities and companies that sell everyday stuff, usually pay dividends. Growth companies usually don't.

In Australia, dividends come with franking credits. That's a tax credit for company tax that's already been paid. A seventy-cent fully franked dividend from CBA, at the thirty percent company tax rate, grosses up to one dollar, with a thirty-cent tax credit you can use to reduce your personal tax. For people in pension-phase super, who pay zero percent tax, those credits can even be refunded as cash. That makes Australian franked dividends worth more than they look. Tax rules can change, so always check the current rules.

**Choice 4: Share buybacks.**
A buyback uses company cash to buy back its own shares and cancel them. Fewer shares means each remaining share is a bigger slice of ownership, so earnings per share rise even if total profit stays flat.

Apple has bought back more than eight hundred billion dollars of its own shares since 2012. Its share count has shrunk from about twenty-six billion to under fifteen billion. As a result, earnings per share grew even faster than profit. Buybacks work best when the stock is undervalued. Doing a buyback when the stock is overpriced destroys value.

The right framework: the best CEOs ask where their money will earn the highest return for the risk. If reinvesting earns twenty-five percent, reinvest. If the stock is undervalued and returns inside the business are only so-so, buy back shares. Don't overpay for acquisitions at the top of the cycle.

Henry Singleton of Teledyne is one of history's most celebrated capital allocators. He issued new shares when his stock was expensive and bought back shares aggressively when it was cheap, and he generated extraordinary returns over the long run. Buffett is another.

**The US dollar.** What moves a currency? Lots of things, but mainly trade and money flows. A currency rises when more people want it, either to buy that country's exports or to invest there. If a country exports a lot, buyers need its currency, so it goes up. Imports work the other way. When the Fed raises rates, investing in the US pays more, money flows in, and the US dollar goes up. And global commodities like oil, wheat and soybeans are all priced in US dollars, so the whole world needs them.

So, to answer the author's own question, is the US dollar driven by policy and trade? Yes, both. Interest-rate policy and money flows usually matter most in the short run. Trade matters over the long run.

### Active vs passive

Now, active versus passive. We know passive funds are ETFs. They brought the cost of investing way down. I'd say don't go for ETFs linked to a theme, like AI, data centres or clean energy. Most of those have done poorly over long periods, because by the time a theme has its own ETF, the excitement is usually already in the price. Instead, go for what they call an index fund. It's just an ETF that buys an index. For example, the S&P 500 holds about 500 of America's biggest, profitable companies, chosen by a committee. Trends come and go, companies get added and removed, but you're always exposed to the biggest companies in the US. As at late October 2026, the S&P 500 sits at about 7,744 points.
> ✏️ Fact-check: was "the S&P 500 buys the 500 biggest stocks"; now ~500 large, profitable US companies selected by an S&P committee (S&P Dow Jones Indices methodology)

### Our practice portfolio

Here's how we'll build our practice portfolio.

Half goes into index funds. That could be the S&P 500, or the Nasdaq 100, or a mix of both, say 30 percent S&P 500 and 20 percent Nasdaq 100.

Now we have the other 50 percent to play with. We'll own 10 stocks to start with. Four large caps, worth more than 100 billion dollars each. Two mid caps, worth 10 to 100 billion. Two small caps. And two wildcards: possible hundred-baggers, stocks that could grow a hundred times. They probably won't, but we'll learn something!

## Chapter 26 – Behavioural Finance: Your Own Worst Enemy

[STORY: Alice looks in a mirror, and her reflection is wearing a villain's cape. Narrator: "Meet your biggest investing enemy."]

Understanding your own brain is just as important as understanding any financial statement. Smart, sensible people keep making terrible investment decisions. It's not because they don't know enough. It's because human brains are wired in ways that work against good investing.

### Loss aversion

Research by Daniel Kahneman and Amos Tversky showed that losses feel about twice as painful as the same-sized gains feel good. Losing a thousand dollars feels roughly as bad as winning two thousand feels good. So most people sell their winners too early, to lock in a gain, and hold their losers too long, to avoid the pain of admitting a loss. This is called the disposition effect. You end up with a portfolio full of your worst ideas and empty of your best ones.

[STORY: Alice drops an ice cream (loss) and is devastated. She's given a second ice cream (gain) and is only mildly happy. A balance scale tips towards the loss, about 2 to 1.]

### Recency bias

We give too much weight to whatever happened recently. After the S&P 500 rose more than 20 percent in both 2023 and 2024, and another 16 percent or so in 2025, many investors assumed it would keep going forever. After a crash, people assume more losses are coming. The irony is that the best time to buy has usually been during big falls, when fear is highest. And the worst time is often at the top, when everyone's excited.

[CHART: S&P 500 yearly returns — as at October 2026 | three tall green bars | data: price return 2023 +24.2%, 2024 +23.3%, 2025 +16.4% (total return incl. dividends 26.3%, 25.0%, 17.9%). Sources: S&P Dow Jones Indices; YCharts]
> ✏️ Fact-check: kept 2023 and 2024 (>20%, correct) and added 2025 (+16.4% price / +17.9% total return) (YCharts)

### Confirmation bias

Once we own a stock and believe in it, we hunt for information that agrees with us and ignore anything that doesn't. Being disciplined enough to change your mind when the evidence changes, and to sell when you're wrong rather than when you feel better, is one of the hardest things in investing.

### Overconfidence

Studies keep showing that individual investors overestimate how good they are at picking stocks and timing the market. The average investor trails the index. It's not bad luck. It's trading costs and poor timing: buying after markets rise and selling after they fall. Most professional fund managers trail the index too. About nine in ten US professional fund managers lagged the S&P 500 over fifteen years.
> ✏️ Fact-check: added the figure: ~90% of US large-cap funds underperformed the S&P 500 over 15 years to end-2025 (SPIVA U.S. Year-End 2025)
[CHART: Can the pros beat the index? — as at October 2026 | ten stick-figure fund managers race an index runner; nine fall behind | data: % of US large-cap funds underperforming S&P 500 to Dec 2025: 10 yrs ~86%, 15 yrs ~90%, 20 yrs ~93% (SPIVA U.S. Year-End 2025)]

### Herding

FOMO, the fear of missing out, pulls more buyers in when markets rise and pushes prices even higher. Fear spreads just as fast when markets fall. The dot-com bubble, the 2008 housing bubble and the 2021 meme stock frenzy were all partly driven by herding.

[STORY: A flock of sheep runs towards a "STOCK GOING UP!" sign, then all turn and run away at a "PRICE FALLING!" sign. One calm sheep (Alice) reads a book.]

### What to do about it

Write down your reason for owning every stock, including the exact conditions under which you'd sell. When those conditions are met, sell, no matter how you feel.

Dollar-cost average: invest a fixed amount on a fixed schedule, whatever the market's doing. That removes the temptation to time the market.

Be most suspicious of your own excitement when markets are at all-time highs. Be least suspicious of yourself when they're down 30 percent.

Read the investors who've thought hardest about psychology: Buffett, Munger, Howard Marks and Peter Lynch.

## Chapter 27 – Common Beginner Mistakes

[STORY: Alice walks a path with eight banana peels. Each one, when stepped on, shows a mistake.]

This chapter could save you more money than any other.

### 1. Buying IPOs at peak excitement

Companies choose to list on the share market, which is called an IPO, when markets are hot and prices are high, because that's when they can raise the most money. The company and its investment bank want to sell you shares at the highest possible price. Many IPOs are bad buys for regular investors. Unless you have a strong, independent reason to believe the business is cheap at the IPO price, be patient and wait for the early ups and downs to settle.

### 2. Trading too often

Every trade costs you. Sometimes there's a brokerage fee, there's always the gap between buying and selling prices, called the bid-ask spread, and there may be capital gains tax. Trade often and these costs pile up into a big drag on your returns. The evidence is overwhelming: most active traders do worse than simply buying an index fund and holding it. Less is more.

### 3. Following tips without research

A stock tip from a friend, a Reddit post, a YouTube video or a finfluencer is not a reason to buy. It's a starting point for research. Before you buy anything, you should be able to explain the business model, its competitive position, the price you're paying, and what would prove you wrong. If you can't explain it clearly, don't buy it.

### 4. Confusing a good company with a good investment

Apple is a great company. Whether it's a great investment at any given moment depends on the price you pay compared with the profits it will make. Buying a wonderful business at a crazy price is still a bad investment. And a so-so business bought at a very low price can be an excellent one.

### 5. Using margin, which means borrowing money to invest

Borrowing to invest makes both gains and losses bigger. Say you borrow 10,000 dollars to invest and the investment falls 50 percent. Now you've lost 5,000 dollars, but you still owe the full 10,000. Margin calls, when your broker forces you to sell at the worst possible moment, have destroyed more wealth than almost anything else. Avoid margin until you have years of experience and really understand your risk.

[STORY: A see-saw: Alice's 10,000 borrowed dollars on one side; the investment shrinks to 5,000 but the "You owe" sign stays at 10,000.]

### 6. Panic-selling in downturns

This is the single most destructive thing you can do in investing: selling shares during a big fall because you can't bear the pain. The S&P 500 has fallen 20 percent or more over 25 times since 1928. Every single time, it eventually recovered to new highs, though some recoveries took years, and after the 1929 crash it took decades. People who sold during the global financial crisis, Covid or the 2022 fall, and didn't buy back, locked in their losses and missed the recovery. If a well-diversified portfolio falls 30 percent, the sensible response is to check your reasons for owning it. Has anything really changed? If not, many long-term investors simply hold, or even buy more.

### 7. Ignoring fees

A 1 percent yearly fee on 100,000 dollars is 1,000 dollars every year. Now imagine 100,000 dollars growing for 20 years at 10 percent a year. With a 0.05 percent fee you'd end up with about 667,000 dollars. With a 1.5 percent fee you'd end up with about 511,000. ### 8. Holding too much of your employer's shares

If you work at a company and also own lots of its shares, your pay and your savings both depend on the same company. If it does badly, you could lose your job and your investment at the same time. Spread it out.

## Chapter 28 – Tax and Structure (Especially for Australians)

[STORY: Alice sells a toy she bought for 10 dollars for 40 dollars at a garage sale. A friendly tax collector stick figure appears holding a little clipboard.]

Tax sounds boring, but it's really just maths about who gets what. Ask Claude to explain your tax, or see an accountant. Every year, we work out our profits and losses, but only when we actually sell. And if we make money, we pay tax on it.

That's the key. It's only on realised money: we only pay tax on a gain when we sell. If we already own a share and it goes up, that's paper money, so there's no tax on it yet. Dividends are different: we're taxed on them in the year we receive them.

In Australia, many dividends come with something special called franking credits, which we'll explain in a moment. It's all simpler than it sounds. Ask Claude to explain it in an easy way, or see an accountant, but you can really understand the process yourself. You just need to take the time!

### Capital gains tax (CGT)

When you sell a share for more than you paid, you've made a capital gain. It's added to your taxable income and taxed at your normal rate. But if you've owned it for more than 12 months, only half the gain counts. That's called the 50 percent CGT discount.

Example: you buy 10,000 dollars of shares and sell them for 40,000 dollars more than a year later. Your gain is 30,000 dollars. At the top tax rate of 47 percent, which includes the Medicare levy, you'd pay 14,100 dollars without the discount. With the discount, only 15,000 dollars is added to your income, so the tax is 7,050 dollars. The discount saves you 7,050 dollars on one sale. That's a powerful reason to hold for the long term.

### Franking credits

Australian companies pay 30 percent company tax before handing out dividends. That tax gets passed on to shareholders as franking credits, which you can use to reduce your own tax.

Say CBA pays a 3 dollar 50 fully franked dividend. Add back the company tax and the gross dividend is 5 dollars, with a 1 dollar 50 franking credit. If your tax rate is 30 percent, the tax on 5 dollars is 1 dollar 50, exactly covered by your credit, so you owe nothing extra. If you're in a retirement-phase super fund paying zero tax, you get the 1 dollar 50 back as cash. That makes Australian fully franked dividends worth more than they first look. In real life, CBA paid 5 dollars and 5 cents a share in dividends for the 2026 financial year, fully franked.

### Superannuation

Earnings inside super are taxed at only 15 percent, and gains on things held more than 12 months at just 10 percent. In retirement, or pension phase, it's zero, completely tax-free, up to a limit. Since July 2026, people with very large super balances, over 3 million dollars, pay extra tax on the earnings above that. Employer contributions are now 12 percent of wages. For most Australians, super is the best tax vehicle they have, especially for long-term growth investing.

> ✏️ Fact-check: added the Division 296 extra tax on balances over $3M (effective 30%; over $10M, 40%) from 1 July 2026 (passed Parliament 10 Mar 2026, SMS Magazine), and the $2.0M pension transfer balance cap

### Self-managed super fund (SMSF)

An SMSF is a super fund you run yourself. You choose the investments, including shares, property and ETFs. Setting it up and keeping it compliant costs real money, so it's usually only worthwhile for larger balances. ASIC has suggested around 500,000 dollars, while industry research says SMSFs can be cost-competitive from about 200,000. Get licensed advice before setting one up.

Here's the core message. Tax is never a reason to make a bad investment. But it is a reason to think carefully about which investments sit inside super and which sit outside, and about how long you hold them.

Know the rules, keep more of what you earn, and let compounding do the heavy lifting!

## Chapter 29 – International Markets: Going Beyond the ASX and S&P 500

[STORY: A paper plane with Alice on board flies from Sydney over a world map, landing on China, India, Southeast Asia and Latin America.]

Pack your bags, we're going global!

If you're an Australian who only invests in the ASX, you have heavy exposure to banks and mining companies, and almost no exposure to the technology and platform businesses that have created most of the world's wealth over the last 30 years. That's a structural risk.

### China

China's economy is about 20 trillion US dollars, the second largest in the world, growing about four and a half percent a year. It has world-class tech companies: Alibaba, Tencent, Meituan and BYD. But there are extra risks.
> ✏️ Fact-check: was "GDP ~$18T"; now ~US$19.4T (2025) and ~$20T (2026 projection), growth ~4.5% (IMF WEO 2025–26)

First, VIE structures. Most Chinese tech companies listed in the US or Hong Kong use something called a variable interest entity. It's a set of contracts that gives foreign investors economic exposure, but not actual legal ownership of the Chinese company. It's never been tested in a major dispute.

Second, regulatory risk. China's tech crackdown from 2020 to 2022 wiped 70 to 80 percent off Alibaba and Meituan, with very little warning.

Third, geopolitical risk. Tension between the US and China has threatened to get Chinese shares kicked off US stock exchanges.

Fourth, audit access. America's audit watchdog, the PCAOB, had very limited access to Chinese audit work for years, and only got proper access in 2022.
> ✏️ Fact-check: added that PCAOB secured full access in Dec 2022 (PCAOB)

None of this means you should avoid China. BYD is one of the most interesting companies in the world. But size your bet to match the extra risk.

### India

India's economy is about 4 trillion US dollars, growing about six and a half to seven percent a year, the fastest of any big economy. It has a young population, a large English-speaking workforce and a growing middle class. The Nifty 50, India's main share index, has returned about 12 percent a year over 20 years in rupees. That's a bit less in Aussie dollars, because the rupee has weakened. Key names include Reliance Industries, HDFC Bank, Infosys and TCS.

The risks are complicated regulation, weaker company governance than in developed markets, and the currency. The rupee has lost about half its value against the US dollar over 20 years, from roughly 45 rupees per dollar to about 88.

### Southeast Asia

Sea Limited does gaming, online shopping and fintech across Southeast Asia. Grab does ride-hailing and food delivery. GoTo is in Indonesia. These are early-stage, high-risk, fast-growing markets.

### Latin America

MercadoLibre, ticker MELI, is the dominant e-commerce and fintech company across Latin America, and one of the most compelling growth stories outside the US. Nubank, ticker NU, is Brazil's digital bank. It now has about 139 million customers across Brazil, Mexico and Colombia, and it beat the old banks with a lower-cost model.

### The key questions

For every international market, ask: can you get audited financial statements you trust? Is there genuine rule of law protecting small shareholders? How good is the corporate governance? These are reasons to demand higher returns for the extra risk, not necessarily reasons to stay away.

The whole world is your playground. Explore it, but with your eyes wide open!

## Glossary of Key Terms

[SCREEN: Each term flips in like a trading card with a little icon.]

ASX: the Australian Securities Exchange, where Australian public companies are listed.

ARPU: average revenue per user. A common measure for subscription and phone companies.

Basis points: one basis point is one hundredth of a percent. Fifty basis points is half a percent. Used for fees and interest rates.

Book value, or equity: assets minus liabilities on the balance sheet. What shareholders own, in theory.

Buyback: when a company buys its own shares on the market, usually cancelling them.

Capex: capital expenditure. Spending on physical or long-lasting assets.

COGS: cost of goods sold. The direct cost of making the product.

DCF: discounted cash flow. A way of valuing a company by working out what all its future cash is worth today.

Dividend: cash paid to shareholders out of profits.

EBIT: earnings before interest and tax.

EBITDA: earnings before interest, tax, depreciation and amortisation.

EPS: earnings per share. Net profit divided by the number of shares. The "E" in PE.

ETF: exchange-traded fund. A basket of investments that trades like a single share.

EV: enterprise value. Market cap plus net debt. Roughly what it would cost to buy the whole business.

FCF: free cash flow. Cash from operations minus capex. The cash a business actually generates.

Forward PE: the PE ratio using expected future earnings, usually for the next 12 months.

Franking credits: tax credits attached to Australian dividends for company tax already paid.

GDP: gross domestic product. The total value of goods and services a country produces in a year.

Gross margin: revenue minus cost of goods sold, divided by revenue. How much of each dollar of sales the company keeps before its other costs.

IPO: initial public offering. When a private company first sells shares to the public.

LTM: last twelve months. Also called trailing twelve months, or TTM.

Market cap: market capitalisation. Share price times the number of shares. The market's total value for the company.

MER: management expense ratio. The yearly fee charged by an ETF or managed fund.

Moat: a lasting competitive advantage that protects a business from competitors, like a moat around a castle.

Net profit, or net income: what's left after all expenses, interest and tax. The "E" in PE.

NTM: next twelve months. A forward-looking earnings estimate.

PE ratio: price-to-earnings ratio. Market cap divided by net profit, or share price divided by EPS.

Present value: what a future amount of money is worth today.

ROIC: return on invested capital. Operating profit after tax divided by the money invested in the business. It measures how well a company uses its capital.

SBC: stock-based compensation. Shares given to employees as part of their pay. A real cost that's often left out of "adjusted" earnings.

SMSF: self-managed super fund. An Australian super fund you run yourself.

Trailing PE: the PE ratio using the last 12 months of actual, audited earnings.

Yield: yearly income, from dividends or interest, divided by the price paid. A 4 percent yield means 4 dollars a year for every 100 dollars invested.

## Final Thought

[STORY: Montage: Alice's lemonade stand, then a workshop, then a small office, then a tall building with her name on it. Fade to Alice looking around her room at everyday objects, each tagged "someone built this".]

The wealthiest people in the world usually have one main asset: shares in a business they built. Investing in the share market lets you share in the growth of other people's businesses. Done patiently and intelligently, it has historically grown wealth over time. But nothing matches what's possible when you build something yourself.

Learn markets, understand business, and use that knowledge to build. Thinking like an investor, understanding how value is created, what makes an advantage last, and what drives earnings, makes you a better entrepreneur, a better manager, and a better user of your own time and energy.

The market is endlessly interesting because it's a mirror of human activity and human psychology. Every number in a financial statement stands for real people making real products, serving real customers and competing against real rivals. Everything around us was built by someone. Don't take any of it for granted.

Stay curious. Keep learning. Question everything. Enjoy the game!
